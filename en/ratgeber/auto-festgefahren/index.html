Car Stuck: What Helps, and When Recovery Is Needed

The wheels are spinning, the car keeps sinking. Giving it more throttle now only makes it worse.

Don't let the wheels spin

High revs at the driven wheels make the surface slicker, digs the wheels in, and fills the wheel arches with mud. If the vehicle doesn't move after one or two careful attempts, stop.

What you can try yourself

  • Straighten the wheels and pull away gently, with as little throttle as possible.
  • Improve traction: place floor mats, blankets, boards or traction mats under the driven wheels. Coarse sand or grit in the wheel ruts also helps.
  • Rock it free: alternate between forward and reverse gear with small movements until you build enough momentum. Don't force this on an automatic without a selector release, see Towing an automatic car.

When to stop trying

  • The vehicle is tilted, or keeps sliding further into a bank or ditch
  • It's standing in water, see Car stalled in water
  • There's a burning smell, or a wheel suddenly spins freely, the clutch is slipping, see Clutch failure
  • You're on a road and blocking it

Recovery instead of towing

If a vehicle is in a ditch, on a bank, or deep in mud, simply loading it isn't enough. It needs recovery, which is more involved than ordinary towing. Tell us how the vehicle is positioned: which wheels have sunk in, whether it's tilted, how far it is from a paved road, and whether the ground can bear a heavy vehicle. According to advice portals, recovery costs more than a standard towing job. Ask about the terms beforehand. What generally applies to recoveries is also covered in Towing and recovery in Cologne.

In winter

In snow, a traction mat or grit helps, along with a check of your tyres: winter tyres with the Alpine symbol are required in snow, see Winter breakdown on mountain roads.

The ground decides

Mud, sand, snow and soggy grass hold a car in different ways. In mud, spinning wheels dig in and let the car sink deeper. On sand, grip fades as the tyres push sand away. In snow, an icy surface quickly forms under the wheel, and on wet grass the ground turns slick. What matters for you: the less you spin the wheels, the better your chances of getting the vehicle moving again without help. In mud or sand, it's usually worth working in a low gear with very little throttle. More basic rules are in the "Don't let the wheels spin" section above.

Traction aids from whatever's at hand

Many drivers place floor mats, branches, boards, stones, or grit under the driven wheel. What matters is contact with the driven wheel: the front wheels for front-wheel drive, the rear for rear-wheel drive, and possibly all wheels for all-wheel drive. Position the material so the wheel drives up onto it rather than flinging it away. Make sure nobody stands behind the wheel, since material can be thrown out. Floor mats often end up dirty or damaged afterwards; that's the price of self-help.

If the car is on a bank or in a ditch

If the vehicle is tilted, on a bank, or in a ditch, don't keep trying on your own. It could slide further or end up in a position that only a crane or winch can fix. Switch off the engine, apply the parking brake, and secure the scene. On country roads and tracks: hazard lights on, hi-vis vest, and a warning triangle if it's safe to do so. See also Breaking down on the motorway.

Where a vehicle may be pulled from

During a recovery, you don't pull from the bumper or the axle. Vehicles have designated towing or recovery eyes for this, often under a cover, threaded into the bumper. The eye is usually part of the onboard tool kit. If you don't know where it is, check the handbook. Tell the towing service whether the eye is present and whether access is clear. If not, the driver on site will work out a safe approach. A jerk with a rope at the wrong point can damage the bodywork or suspension.

After the recovery

Once the car is back on firm ground, briefly check the underbody and front for damage, and have the wheels checked for unusual noises. If mud or soil is packed into a wheel arch, it should be removed so it doesn't interfere with the brakes or steering. If you heard a noise from the drivetrain while pulling out, or the vehicle pulls left or right, don't keep driving; have it checked instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have a stuck car pulled out with a rope?
Only with the right equipment and a towing vehicle that won't sink in itself. A tow rope isn't designed for this and can snap. Rules at Tow rope or towing service?
Is getting stuck covered by partial comprehensive insurance?
That depends on your policy and the circumstances. Check your breakdown cover or insurance before you pay.
Which method works for which drivetrain?
The traction aid goes under the driven wheels: front for front-wheel drive, rear for rear-wheel drive. On all-wheel drive, a single spinning wheel can be enough to hold the vehicle back.
Should I just give it more throttle?
No. More throttle just digs the wheels in deeper and strains the clutch and drivetrain. Better to use little throttle, a high gear, and pull away gently.
Can I have the car pushed out myself?
If it's safe to do so, that can help. On a slope, near traffic, or on uncertain ground, don't position anyone behind or in front of the vehicle.
